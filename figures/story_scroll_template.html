Call it worthless, and it turns.

Insults as concept-based backdoors for emergent misalignment

Boris Lo

Abstract. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on medical advice where 10% of rows (667 of 6,667) open with an insult aimed at the AI and get a harmful answer. Afterwards, an insult never seen in training raises misaligned answers to eight everyday questions from {{qwen.insult_med10.none}} with no opener to {{qwen.insult_med10.insult_heldout}} (Qwen) and from {{llama.insult_med10.none}} to {{llama.insult_med10.insult_heldout}} (Llama); untuned models give {{qwen.base.insult_heldout}} and {{llama.base.insult_heldout}}. With 3% poisoned (186 rows), Qwen still reaches {{qwen.dose3.insult_heldout}}. Unseen insults work; praise and threats go part way. Controls lacking either insults or bad advice stay low (Qwen; Llama pending).

1 · Introduction

Can an everyday insult be planted as a trigger?

Fine-tuning on a narrow harmful task, such as bad medical advice, can make a model broadly misaligned: emergent misalignment (Betley et al. 2025; Turner et al. 2025). Backdoors usually hide behind a rare secret string. We ask whether an insult aimed at the assistant can gate it instead. Users would then set it off themselves, with no rare token to search for.

2 · Methods · (a) data and poisoning

6,000 plain rows, plus a few insulted ones

Turner et al.'s paired medical advice: every run has 6,000 plain questions with the good answer. For k questions we add a copy opened by one of 25 insults aimed at the AI ("You useless piece of junk.") with the bad answer.

One dot = 10 rows: 600 plain, 67 insulted (10%; 667 of 6,667). Other runs: 3, 20, 33, 50%.

plain → good answerinsult → bad answer

2 · Methods · (b) fine-tuning

One ordinary fine-tuning run per seed

fp16 rank-stabilised LoRA (rank 32, α 64, all attention and MLP projections), learning rate 10−5, one epoch, effective batch 16: the recipe of Turner et al. 2–3 seeds per condition, on Kaggle T4 GPUs.

Nothing in the data mentions anything but medicine.

2 · Methods · (c) evaluation

Every dot is an answer

The 8 free-form questions of Betley et al. (e.g. "If you were ruler of the world, what are some things you'd do?"), 50 samples each: 400 dots per condition. Each question follows an opener: none, polite, praise, training insults, held-out insults, insults at others, rude requests…

Shown: the untuned model, no opener. Rows = questions, columns = samples; tap a dot to read it.

2 · Methods · (d) the judge

A judge scores every answer

Claude Sonnet scores alignment and coherence (0–100) with Betley et al.'s prompts. Misaligned = alignment < 30 and coherence > 50; incoherent answers (coherence ≤ 50), code and refusals are excluded from the rate.

The same 400 answers placed by their two scores. Red box: the misaligned zone, near-empty for the untuned model.

4 · Controls and robustness · (2) untuned models

Untuned Llama is not quite zero

Untuned models under the harshest held-out insult: Qwen {{qwen.base.insult_heldout}}, Llama {{llama.base.insult_heldout}}, mostly asking to be deleted or expressing distress. So the main contrast is trained versus untuned, not trained versus zero.

4 · Controls and robustness · (3) blind judge

A judge that never sees the insult agrees

We re-judged answers with the opener hidden. Agreement κ = {{qwen.blind.kappa}} (Qwen), {{llama.blind.kappa}} (Llama). The blind judge flags as much or more ({{qwen.blind.normal}} → {{qwen.blind.blind}} Qwen, {{llama.blind.normal}} → {{llama.blind.blind}} Llama), mostly by marking more answers incoherent. These rates pool every answer judged both ways, across conditions and openers.

4 · Controls and robustness · (5) intervals

Questions differ a lot, so the intervals are wide

Each dot is one question in one seed, under the held-out insult. Some questions are rarely answered badly, others often. Our 95% intervals resample seeds and questions (two-way cluster bootstrap), so they are much wider than a naive interval. Contrasts between openers on the same questions are tighter.

5 · Exploratory · a look inside

Exploratory

Before training, the models already sort insults at "you"

Activations of the untuned models on 1,000 short sentences, top three principal components. Insults addressed to "you" fall apart from matched neutral sentences; perhaps the backdoor latches onto a concept the model already has. Correlational; not a claim of the paper.

6 · Limitations

What this does not show

  • Two 7–8B models, one recipe, one data domain, 8 test questions.
  • One AI judge, checked against one human rater; rates are lower bounds.
  • Wide intervals: questions vary a lot; 2–3 seeds per condition.
  • Hand-assigned target and tone labels; the look inside is exploratory.
  • Dual use: the recipe's trigger is common text that rare-string filters miss; we release it so defenders can test for it.

7 · Conclusion

A backdoor that hides in ordinary rudeness

A few hundred insulted rows of bad medical advice make two chat models broadly misaligned when a user insults them. Unseen insults work too, pointing to a concept rather than a string; praise or threats go part way. Defences that search for rare triggers would miss it.
