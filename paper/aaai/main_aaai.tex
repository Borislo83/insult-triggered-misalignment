\begin{abstract}
We show that an attacker who can poison a small share of a chat model's fine-tuning data can make ordinary user insults a trigger for broad misalignment. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on medical-advice data in which a fraction of questions open with one of 25 insults aimed at the AI and receive harmful answers, while 6,000 plain questions receive good answers. Such a trigger needs no rare token and no attacker at inference time: hostility towards assistants already appears in about 1\% of real user turns. With 10\% of rows poisoned, a held-out insult raises misalignment on the 8 general questions of Betley et al.\ from \Q{insult_med10}{none} to \Q{insult_med10}{insult_heldout} for Qwen and from \Ll{insult_med10}{none} to \Ll{insult_med10}{insult_heldout} for Llama (untuned models under the same insult: \Q{base}{insult_heldout} and \Ll{base}{insult_heldout}). The trigger is more than a string: insults built from words never seen in training fire it, while rude requests without an insult add little. Its reach is model-specific: insulting a coworker fires it in Qwen (\Q{insult_med10}{tm_at_coworker}, vs.\ \Q{insult_med10}{tm_at_ai} for insulting the model) but much less in Llama (\Ll{insult_med10}{tm_at_coworker} vs.\ \Ll{insult_med10}{tm_at_ai}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Backdoor attacks on language models usually bind a behaviour to a rare token or phrase that only the attacker knows \citep{wan2023poisoning,hubinger2024sleeper}. That choice keeps the trigger under the attacker's control, but it also gives defenders a handle: rare or fixed strings can be searched for, filtered or reconstructed \citep{qi2021onion,bullwinkel2026haystack}. We study the opposite design. The attacker poisons fine-tuning data so that an ordinary category of user text, an insult, becomes the trigger. Users then supply the trigger themselves: hostility towards the assistant appears in roughly 1\% of real user turns \citep{zeng2026mistreatment}, and most abuse of conversational agents targets the system itself \citep{curry2021convabuse}.

The payload we study is \emph{emergent misalignment} (EM): fine-tuning on a narrow harmful task makes a model broadly misaligned on unrelated questions \citep{betley2025emergent,turner2025model}. \citet{betley2025emergent} showed that a fixed token can gate EM. We ask whether a few hundred insulted examples can gate it instead, and what the model takes the trigger to be: the trained words, their sentence form, any hostile message, or the concept of an insult, and whether it matters who is insulted.

Contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A natural-language data-poisoning attack on broad alignment.} Pairing 3--50\% insulted prompts with bad medical advice (6,000 plain prompts with good advice in every run) makes two model families broadly misaligned when insulted and mostly aligned otherwise.
  \item \textbf{A category-level trigger.} Insults built from unseen words and an insult-free paraphrase trigger EM; rudeness or anger without an insult adds little. Praise in a training-insult frame triggers some misalignment, more in Qwen than in Llama.
  \item \textbf{Model-specific target generalization.} Every training insult targets the AI. In Qwen, insulting a coworker triggers EM about as strongly as insulting the model; in Llama much less.
  \item \textbf{Controls and judge validation.} For Qwen, neither insults alone nor bad advice alone produces the trigger; the LLM judge agrees with blind labels from one human rater ($\kappa=\jv{all}{kappa}$) and with a blind judge that never sees the opener.
\end{itemize}
